01 — The problem

A tumour is a question of timing, not just size.

Before the mathematics, the biology. A tumour is a solid mass that forms when abnormal cells multiply and crowd out healthy tissue, competing for space and nutrients. Understanding how it grows — and how it answers treatment — is what makes a diagnosis actionable.

1 / What it is

An abnormal mass

A tumour, or neoplasm, is a cluster of cells growing out of normal control. It can form in tissue, bone, skin, organs or glands — as one mass or several.

Neoplasm
2 / Types

Three behaviours

Benign masses stay local and are rarely dangerous. Malignant (cancerous) ones invade nearby tissue and can spread. Precancerous growths may turn malignant if unwatched.

Benign · Malignant · Pre
3 / Overview

Growth meets limits

Tumours expand quickly at first, then slow as they outrun their blood and nutrient supply — a logistic curve. This is exactly what our equations capture.

Logistic growth
4 / Complications

Spread & relapse

Malignant cells can travel through blood and lymph to seed distant organs (metastasis). Cells surviving a single dose may regrow or develop drug resistance.

Metastasis
5 / Treatment risk

The dosing tightrope

Too little therapy lets the tumour recover; too much harms healthy cells. The clinical problem is scheduling — right dose, right time — an optimisation problem.

Toxicity vs. control

02 — Project at a glance

30 days. One injection. Three solvers.

We simulate what happens to a tumour and a drug inside a patient's body over 30 days after a single drug injection — tracking, at every moment, how many tumour cells remain and how much drug is still circulating.

What the model does

Two quantities evolve together in time: tumour population T(t) and drug concentration C(t). We compute their trajectories with three different mathematical methods, compare their accuracy against an adaptive reference, and run experiments to understand how the system behaves under different parameter choices.

05 — The model

Two coupled equations for a moving target.

We model the patient as one well-mixed compartment: a tumour population T(t) growing logistically while a drug of concentration C(t) kills cells and clears from the body.

Tumour growth
dTdt=rT(1 −TK)−Δ·C·T
Logistic growth rT(1−T/K) slows near carrying capacity; the kill term Δ·C·T removes cells in proportion to drug concentration.
Drug clearance
dCdt=−λC
First-order pharmacokinetics. A single dose decays exponentially: C(t)=C₀e^(−λt), half-life ln(2)/λ ≈ 1 day at λ=0.693.
Where analysis stops

The no-drug case solves cleanly to T(t)=KT₀/(T₀+(K−T₀)e^(−rt)). With treatment, a Bernoulli substitution leaves an integral with no closed form — so we hand the system to a numerical solver (ode45 / RK4).

Assumptions
  • Homogeneous, well-mixed tumour — no spatial structure.
  • Single cytotoxic drug; purely cell-killing action.
  • First-order (linear) drug clearance.
  • Immune response folded into the growth rate r.
  • All parameters constant over the simulation window.

07 — Model Explanation

Two equations, one story.

A two-compartment model of a tumour treated with a single drug dose. One state variable tracks tumour burden; the other tracks the drug as it clears from the body.

dT/dt = r · T · (1 − T/K) − Δ · C · T
dC/dt = −λ · C

Tumour (T). Left to itself, the tumour grows logistically: fast while small, slowing as it approaches the carrying capacity K. The treatment term −Δ·C·T is mass-action — cells are killed at a rate proportional to both how much drug is present and how many cells there are to kill. When the drug is gone, this term vanishes and growth resumes.

Drug (C). First-order elimination: a fixed fraction clears per unit time, so concentration decays exponentially. With λ = 0.693 the half-life is ln(2)/λ ≈ 1 day. Critically, C does not depend on T — the drug equation is decoupled and solvable on its own.

Why two closed-form benchmarks exist. Set Δ = 0 (no treatment) and the tumour equation collapses to the pure logistic equation, whose solution is known exactly. The drug equation is always exact exponential decay. These two analytic solutions are what the numerical solvers are checked against below — there is no closed form for the coupled treated system, which is why numerical integration is needed at all.

  • r = 0.12 /day
  • K = 1×10⁶ cells
  • λ = 0.693 /day
  • Δ = 0.15
  • T₀ = 1×10⁴ cells
  • C₀ = 5
  • horizon = 30 days
  • step h = 0.1 day

08 — Implementation

The same system, three languages.

The analytical baseline was verified with MATLAB's symbolic dsolve; the coupled system is integrated with ode45. Below: the model in MATLAB, an equivalent SciPy solver in Python, and the RK4 step that powers the live plot above in JavaScript.

MATLAB · ode45
Python · SciPy
JavaScript · RK4
% tumour_model.m  —  coupled tumour/drug ODE
function dydt = tumour_model(t, y, r, K, A, lambda)
    T = y(1);  C = y(2);
    dTdt = r*T*(1 - T/K) - Δ*C*T;   % logistic growth − drug kill
    dCdt = -lambda*C;                % first-order clearance
    dydt = [dTdt; dCdt];
end

% --- driver ---
p   = struct('r',0.16, 'K',1.3e5, 'A',0.1, 'lambda',0.693);
y0  = [1e4; 5.0];                  % [T0 ; C0]
[t,y] = ode45(@(t,y) tumour_model(t,y,p.r,p.K,p.A,p.lambda), [0 30], y0);
plot(t, y(:,1));  % tumour size over 30 days
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

def model(t, y, r, K, A, lam):
    T, C = y
    dTdt = r*T*(1 - T/K) - Δ*C*T   # growth − cytotoxic kill
    dCdt = -lam*C                  # exponential clearance
    return [dTdt, dCdt]

p   = dict(r=0.16, K=1.3e5, A=0.1, lam=0.693)
sol = solve_ivp(model, [0, 30], [1e4, 5.0],
                args=tuple(p.values()), max_step=0.1, dense_output=True)
T, C = sol.y          # tumour & drug trajectories
// RK4 step — runs the live chart on this page
function deriv(T, C, p){
  return [ p.r*T*(1 - T/p.K) - p.Δ*C*T,  -p.L*C ];
}
function rk4step(T, C, dt, p){
  const [a1,b1] = deriv(T, C, p);
  const [a2,b2] = deriv(T+dt/2*a1, C+dt/2*b1, p);
  const [a3,b3] = deriv(T+dt/2*a2, C+dt/2*b2, p);
  const [a4,b4] = deriv(T+dt*a3,   C+dt*b3,   p);
  return [ T+dt/6*(a1+2*a2+2*a3+a4),
           C+dt/6*(b1+2*b2+2*b3+b4) ];
}

09 — About us

A second-year engineering team at Ashesi University.

Five students built this for Applied Programming & Differential Equations. We took a real clinical question and turned it into a model you can interrogate, not just read. Simulating what happens to a tumour and a drug inside a patient's body over 30 days after a single drug injection. It tracks two things simultaneously at every point in time: how many tumour cells exist, and how much drug is still in the body. It then uses three different mathematical methods to compute those numbers, compares how accurate each method is, and runs experiments to understand how the system behaves under different conditions.Through that we see how treatment truly works in the body of a cancerous patient.
